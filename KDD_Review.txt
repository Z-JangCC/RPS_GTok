Paper Summary:
This paper proposes RPS-GTok, a graph tokenizer designed for reconstructability and permutation stability. The method combines graph canonical relabeling, primitive graph-editing operations, and merging of recurring primitive fragments. It evaluates tokenization across 21 graph datasets and compares with multiple kinds of baselines.

Paper Strengths:
The paper targets a meaningful and well-defined problem on graph tokenization.

The overall design is coherent in a high level. Canonicalization removes dependence on arbitrary node IDs, primitive operations provide an explicit reconstruction path, and vocabulary-based fragments provide compression. The relationship between these components is clear.

The experimental coverage is broad, spanning 21 datasets and multiple tokenizers.

Paper Weaknesses:
My concerns are mostly on the rigorousness of experiment settings.

The downstream evaluation is restricted to topology surrogate tasks, like density bins and triangle existence. Given multiple datasets contain their domain-specific tasks (e.g., node classification, link prediction), it is important to evaluate the proposed method on those tasks as well to demonstrate the end-to-end capabilities and advantages of the method.

The baseline methods were truncated to a 512-token budget. Some of the downstream advantage may come from sequence truncation rather than better representations. Similarly, RPS-GTok (Full-Embed) is not directly comparable to the token-only baselines, as it supplies the model with additional features.

Runtime/scaling results are absent, which are important metrics especially on canonical relabeling.

Plus, the author should spend more effort demonstrating the novelty of their work, compared with a combination of existing techniques. A deeper motivation (or illustrating example) might help.

Paper Summary:
This paper proposes RPS-GTok, a tokenizer that encodes graphs into discrete token sequences, aiming to satisfy both reconstructability (tokens expand back into a graph equivalent to the source under a prescribed schema) and permutation stability (relabeling nodes leaves the emitted sequence unchanged). The method is a four-stage pipeline: attributed canonical relabeling removes dependence on input node identifiers; the canonical graph is compiled into an ordered program of graph-editing primitives; order-preserving subsequences of that program, induced on candidate node sets constrained by size and boundary ports, are matched against an offline frozen vocabulary and retained greedily; tokens carrying an identifier and a local payload are emitted, with the identifier consumed by the Transformer and vocabulary-uncovered content falling back to primitive entries. Experiments cover token cost on 21 datasets, a comparison against nine direct graph representations on 2,602 sampled graphs, and classification and regression on four datasets with topology-derived targets.

Paper Strengths:
Defining a token as an executable primitive fragment rather than a latent code or a text substring is the core concept separating this work from every compared method. Each token carries an expansion map, primitives carry local preconditions, and invalid programs are rejected as a whole, so a token's meaning can be checked by execution.
The fallback design ensures that no graph admitted by the schema is unencodable. Edge-level and isolated-node candidates remain admissible and uncovered content is emitted as primitives, so limited vocabulary coverage lengthens the sequence instead of dropping graph content.
The graph schema uniformly handles cases most graph serialization work sidesteps, including multiedges, self-loops, isolated nodes, directed edges, and node and edge attributes.
The related-work section organizes existing methods into four classes by the graph-to-token pathway with a consistent criterion, which is useful to readers entering this area.
Paper Weaknesses:
W1 (fundamental): The two claimed properties amount to computing a canonical form, which has no known polynomial-time algorithm and is at least as hard as graph isomorphism

The two claimed properties (Eq. 30, Eq. 34) require the token sequences of two graphs to be equal if and only if the graphs are isomorphic, so any tokenizer satisfying both must compute some canonical form. Conversely, composing a canonical form with any invertible serialization yields both properties at once, and the gap this paper describes disappears. Canonization is no easier than isomorphism testing, since given canonical forms isomorphism is decided trivially, and the latter has no known polynomial-time algorithm [R1]. Existing serializations skip canonical relabeling precisely because of this cost.

Here the refinement step (Eq. 12) is one-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman, which cannot separate regular graphs; once it stalls, the procedure falls back to an individualization-refinement search (Appendix A, following [33]), a paradigm with a known exponential lower bound [R2]. Table 1 contains many near-regular or attribute-sparse graphs: the superpixel datasets, roadNet-CA, IEEE Power Grid.

The paper gives neither a complexity analysis nor any runtime: Eq. (35) covers only compilation and fragment formation, and canonical relabeling is dispatched in one sentence in Appendix B. This also makes Table 2 uninformative, since comparing token length across complexity classes establishes nothing. The complexity of graph isomorphism and canonical labeling is well settled in the literature, yet the paper cites none of it and does not identify the nature of the problem it poses. I would encourage the authors to cover this literature and to analyze each stage with upper and lower bounds.

W2 (fundamental): The encoding is invertible, but invertibility is lost once a generative model is attached, and no path to extending it is given

Paper Summary:
This paper proposes RPS-GTok, a graph tokenizer designed to provide reconstructable and permutation-stable graph token sequences. It first canonicalizes the graph, compiles it into primitive graph-editing operations, merges recurring primitive fragments using a learned vocabulary, and emits tokens that can be expanded back into the canonical graph.

Paper Strengths:
Well-motivated problem: Reconstructability and permutation stability are useful properties for graph tokenization, especially when graph representations are consumed by sequence models.

Approach: The primitive-operation representation provides an explicit reconstruction path rather than relying on latent embeddings, and the paper gives formal properties for reconstruction and permutation stability.

Token-level evaluation: The paper did a pretty through evaluations.

Paper Weaknesses:
Baselines: The paper compares against edge lists, adjacency lists, GraphML, etc., and argues that these methods are reconstructable but not permutation-stable. However, RPS-GTok obtains permutation stability largely through graph canonical relabeling. A much stronger and more direct baseline would be the same canonical relabeling followed by an edge list, adjacency list, or other standard reversible serialization. Such a baseline could also achieve both reconstruction and permutation stability, and would better isolate whether the proposed primitive-fragment tokenization itself provides meaningful benefit. The current comparison makes the joint reconstruction stability result less convincing.

The token-efficiency metric does not measure the complete reconstructable representation: The reported Token/edge and Token/node measure only the model-facing token identifiers, while reconstruction additionally requires token-specific payloads containing ports, attributes, and other expansion arguments. The paper explicitly acknowledges that the lossless footprint depends on these payloads, but does not include their size in the main efficiency comparison. So claims about compact reconstructable tokenization should also report the total storage or serialized size required for reconstruction.

Evaluations: The Transformer experiments predict topology-derived targets such as node count, edge count, density, and triangle count rather than the original semantic labels of the graph datasets. These tasks naturally favor a tokenizer explicitly designed to retain exact topology. I think evaluation on real graph classification, graph reasoning, or generation tasks would be more convincing evidence that the tokenizer benefits downstream Transformer models.

Scalability of canonicalization: The paper acknowledges that canonical relabeling may require individualization-refinement search and can dominate runtime on highly symmetric graphs, without a uniform polynomial-time guarantee. However, no systematic runtime or memory comparison is provided. Since canonicalization is required before every tokenization, this is important for establishing scalability to large graphs.

Paper Summary:
This paper proposes RPS-GTok, a graph tokenization framework designed for Transformer-based models, with the goal of achieving both reconstructability and permutation stability. Specifically, RPS-GTok first applies canonical relabeling to mitigate the influence of arbitrary node identifiers on graph serialization. It then compiles each graph into an executable sequence of primitive operations and constructs a graph vocabulary from the training data to compress frequently occurring primitive fragments into compact graph tokens. Structural patterns not covered by the vocabulary are retained through the underlying primitive operations, thereby preserving complete graph information and ensuring reconstructability. Experiments on 21 graph datasets evaluate the tokenization overhead, while tests on 2,602 sampled graphs demonstrate 100% reconstruction accuracy and permutation stability. In addition, the authors evaluate RPS-GTok on multiple topology classification and regression tasks using a fixed Transformer architecture. The results show that RPS-GTok provides compact token sequences while effectively preserving graph topological information and achieving competitive downstream performance.

Paper Strengths:
1. The research problem is clearly motivated and practically relevant. Graphs lack an inherent linear ordering, whereas Transformers are sensitive to the order of input tokens. Therefore, constructing graph token sequences that preserve topological information while remaining invariant to arbitrary node IDs is a well-motivated and important problem.

2. The proposed method is systematically designed. The overall framework is well structured, with its key components jointly addressing graph serialization, token compression, reconstructability, and permutation stability.

3. The tokenizer-level evaluation is comprehensive. The paper provides extensive tokenizer-level experiments, covering token efficiency, graph reconstruction, and permutation stability across a diverse set of graph datasets.

Paper Weaknesses:
1. The compactness evaluation is incomplete. The reported token cost mainly accounts for token IDs, while graph reconstruction additionally relies on auxiliary payload information that is not fully reflected in the compression analysis.

2. The downstream evaluation is relatively limited. The evaluated tasks are primarily based on predicting graph topological statistics, with limited validation on more realistic graph learning tasks.

3. The scalability analysis is insufficient. The computational efficiency of canonical relabeling on large-scale or highly symmetric graphs has not been thoroughly evaluated.
